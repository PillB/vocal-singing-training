## How strong the evidence is (checked 2026-10-28)

- **SOVT.** The mechanism (back pressure from a narrowed tract) is well described (Titze 2006, J Speech Lang Hear Res 49). Evidence that the exercises improve voices over time is thin: a meta-analysis of 8 studies in dysphonic adults found SOVT therapy "is not to be considered significantly superior" to other treatments (Pozzali et al. 2024, J Voice 38(1)), and a 2025 review of straw work found three trials, each at high risk of bias (Asadollahpour et al., J Voice, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2025.01.010). Treat s4, s6 and s27 as standard warm-ups, not as proven training.
- **Fillers.** Fillers are not pure noise. Hearing *uh* helped listeners recognise the next words, and *um* neither helped nor hurt (Fox Tree 2001, Memory & Cognition 29(2)); fillers and rising endings did lower listeners' sense of how much the speaker knew (Brennan & Williams 1995, J Memory and Language 34(3)). That supports *fewer* fillers in a talk, not "kill" them: v11's own step ("fewer fillers each round, not perfection") is the defensible version.
- **Pauses.** Listeners remembered words that came after a silent pause better (MacGregor, Corley & Donaldson 2010, Neuropsychologia 48(14)). That is about pauses *before* a word; that pauses add authority or credibility is coaching lore, not a finding. In read speech in five languages including Spanish, silent pauses ran up to about 1 s; pauses over 1 s appeared only in the spontaneous (French) corpus (Campione & Véronis, Speech Prosody 2002), so v10's 0.8–3 s pause is a deliberate device, not a norm.
